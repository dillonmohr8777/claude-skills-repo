# Manufacturing industry brief

One line: manufacturers are proud of what they build and terrible at telling anyone outside the plant, and the buyer researching them online usually gives up before they ever pick up the phone.

## Who the buyer is

Two buyers matter here, the manufacturer we sell to, and the manufacturer's own buyer (the engineer or procurement lead they need to reach).

- **Owner or GM of a small-to-midsize manufacturer** (often family-owned, often multi-generation). Proud of the shop floor, skeptical of marketing spend they can't directly trace to a PO.
- **VP of Sales or Business Development** at larger shops, usually the one who first raises "our website looks like it's from 2012."
- **Marketing manager**, if the company has one, frequently a department of one, juggling trade shows, a Thomasnet listing, and whatever the website vendor left behind.

What they are worried about, in their own frame: a $150,000 deal that came in organically because a buyer found them through search (BigOrange manufacturing hub page, fetched 2026-09-02) is the kind of story that gets an owner's attention, most manufacturers assume that only happens through the trade show circuit or an existing relationship, not through their own website.

## Their customer's journey (who the manufacturer sells to, and how those buyers research)

The manufacturer's buyer is an engineer, procurement specialist, or plant manager sourcing a part, material, or service, often under time pressure and often starting the search online before ever calling a rep.

- Thomasnet (thomasnet.com) reports it attracts over 1.4 million monthly buyers seeking new suppliers across 80,000+ categories on the Thomas Network (business.thomasnet.com, fetched 2026-09-02), a direct signal that industrial buyers are actively searching online at scale, not exclusively working from an existing supplier Rolodex.
- The same Gartner finding that applies to MSP buyers applies here: B2B buyers broadly prefer a rep-free research experience (61% in Gartner's 2025 survey of 632 B2B buyers) and actively avoid suppliers who send irrelevant outreach (73%), source: Gartner survey via Demand Gen Report, published 2025-07-01, fetched 2026-09-02. An industrial buyer researching a supplier is doing the same self-serve, low-trust-in-outreach research any other B2B buyer does.
- Spec sheets, certifications, and capability lists need to be crawlable and findable, an engineer sourcing a part is often searching a very specific term (a material spec, a tolerance, a certification) rather than a category name.

## What is usually broken

1. **Website**, built to look like a brochure, not built to answer "can this shop do the specific thing I need." Capabilities buried in a PDF instead of a page.
2. **Proof**, few or no case studies with real numbers. The $150,000 organic deal (BigOrange hub page, fetched 2026-09-02) is a rare example of a manufacturer being willing to publish a dollar figure, most say nothing at all.
3. **Positioning**, "quality, reliability, on-time delivery" said by every shop in the directory. No specific capability, tolerance, material, or industry named up front.
4. **Lead capture**, an RFQ form with no context about what happens next, no phone number visible, no way to send a quick spec question without committing to a full quote request.
5. **Sales handoff**, inbound RFQs land in a shared inbox instead of a CRM; nobody tracks which one turned into a shipped part.
6. **Measurement**, no attribution between "we sponsored this trade show" and "we closed this account," which keeps manufacturing marketing budgets defensive and small.

## Objections and answers

- **"Our buyers find us through trade shows and existing relationships, not the internet."** Increasingly not true even for legacy accounts, the AMS case study on BigOrange's own site describes a sales-qualified lead arriving the day after launch (fetched 2026-09-02), and the $150,000 organic deal shows a first-time inbound buyer, not an existing relationship.
- **"We tried a website before and it didn't bring in business."** Ask what the site actually said about capabilities and whether it was built to be found for the specific parts/services the shop makes, or just to look presentable.
- **"Marketing is a cost we can't tie to revenue."** That is the measurement-ladder conversation, attribution is buildable; it starts with tagging every inbound RFQ with a source.
- **"Our industry is too niche/technical for content marketing to matter."** Niche is an advantage for search, not a disqualifier, a specific tolerance or material spec page has far less competition than a generic "custom manufacturing" page.

## Proof patterns that work

- A specific dollar figure tied to a specific deal (the $150,000 organically-sourced deal, BigOrange hub page, fetched 2026-09-02) lands harder with an owner than a percentage.
- A fast-turnaround story (a sales-qualified lead the day after launch, BigOrange hub page, fetched 2026-09-02) answers the skeptical "does this even work" objection quickly.
- Certifications and named capabilities (ISO, AS9100, specific materials or tolerances) function as proof in this vertical the way client logos function in agency work, they are checkable facts, not marketing claims.
- Named third-party recognition (Best in Cincy, Expertise Cincinnati, HubSpot/Google Partner, BigOrange hub page, fetched 2026-09-02) works as agency-level proof of competence when pitching the manufacturer, separate from proof the manufacturer shows its own buyers.

## KPIs and measurement ladder

1. **Visibility**, rankings for spec/capability terms (material + process + tolerance), directory presence (Thomasnet and similar).
2. **Traffic**, organic sessions to capability and industry-specific landing pages.
3. **Engagement**, spec sheet downloads, capability page depth, time on RFQ page.
4. **RFQ volume**, form submissions and calls, tagged by source in the CRM.
5. **RFQ quality**, quote-to-close rate, average deal size.
6. **Revenue**, closed-won attributed pipeline, cost per acquired account.

Always state the source, date, and gap on any number quoted to a prospect, never present a directional estimate as a measured result.

## Seasonality and market context

- The Deloitte 2026 Manufacturing Industry Outlook (published 2025-11-13, fetched 2026-09-02) reports the ISM manufacturing index stayed below 50 (contraction territory) for much of 2025, manufacturing employment declined during the year, and over 75% of manufacturers cited trade uncertainty as their top concern in NAM surveys. This is the honest headwind context for any 2026 manufacturing pitch, lead generation matters more, not less, when the top line is under pressure.
- The same Deloitte outlook reports manufacturers expect input costs to rise an average of 5.4% over the next year, and 80% of manufacturing executives plan to invest 20% or more of improvement budgets into smart manufacturing initiatives (fetched 2026-09-02), useful for framing marketing spend as an efficiency investment alongside the plant-floor investment they are already planning.
- Trade show cycles (IMTS in Chicago on its biennial schedule, regional NAM and industry-specific shows) still drive real seasonality in manufacturing marketing budgets and content calendars, treat specific show dates as needing a fresh check per year rather than hardcoding here.

## Compliance cautions

- Never state a specific certification (ISO 9001, AS9100, ITAR, etc.) on a manufacturer's behalf unless verified against their own documentation, this is a legal/compliance claim, not a marketing one.
- Do not quote industry-wide financial figures (deal sizes, ROI percentages) as if they apply to a specific prospect, the $150,000 deal and similar named results are BigOrange's own case studies, not industry averages.
- Manufacturing marketing content that touches export, defense, or government contracts requires the client's own legal/compliance sign-off before publishing, flag and route around, don't draft compliance language.
- Be careful with sustainability/quality claims ("zero-defect," "eco-friendly"), these require the manufacturer's own substantiation, not agency-supplied language.
