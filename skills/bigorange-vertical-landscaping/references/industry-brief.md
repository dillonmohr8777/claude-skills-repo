# Landscaping vertical: industry brief

One-line summary: landscaping owners are swamped with work and starved for a
system. The marketing problem is almost never "not enough people want a
nicer yard," it's "we can't prove we're worth calling back."

## Who the buyer is

Three buyer shapes show up under one "landscaping company" label. Don't pitch
them the same way.

- **Design-build firms.** Sell an experience and a transformation: patios,
  outdoor kitchens, pools, full property overhauls. Ticket sizes run high
  four figures to six figures. The owner sells on trust and portfolio, thinks
  in projects not routes, and the sales cycle is weeks to months with a
  design consultation up front.
- **Maintenance and lawn care companies.** Sell a recurring route: mowing,
  fertilization, cleanups. Ticket sizes are small and the business lives or
  dies on route density and retention, not one-off wins. The owner thinks in
  seasons and routes, and churn (a customer canceling, or going to a
  cheaper mow-and-blow competitor) is the quiet killer of the P&L.
  (`unverified` for any specific churn rate; no sourced figure found this
  research pass.)
- **Hardscape / outdoor-living specialists.** A design-build subtype worth
  calling out on its own: pavers, retaining walls, fire features, lighting,
  irrigation. Often subcontract to design-build firms *and* sell direct,
  which means their marketing has to work for both a homeowner and a
  contractor referral partner.
- **Commercial / grounds crews.** Sell to property managers and HOAs, not
  homeowners. Buying committee, RFP-driven, contract renewal cycles, and
  the sales trigger is usually "our current vendor missed something" rather
  than a Google search.

Across all four, BigOrange's own service page (`landscaping-marketing-services/`,
seen 2026-09-02) frames the owner's actual worry plainly: keeping "your
project pipeline full and your people busy." That's the buyer's real
question, not "how do I get more traffic," but "how do I keep my crews busy
without babysitting the phone myself."

## Their customer's journey

- Residential buyers research before they call. BigOrange's own page states
  "81 percent of homeowners research online before hiring a landscaping
  company" (bigorange.marketing/landscaping-marketing-services/, seen
  2026-09-02); treat this as BigOrange's claimed figure, not independently
  re-verified this pass, and label it accordingly if it goes in front of a
  client without a primary source.
- Reviews carry real weight in that research. BrightLocal's 2026 Local
  Consumer Review Survey (brightlocal.com, seen 2026-09-02) found 97% of
  consumers read reviews when picking a local business, 68% require at
  least a 4-star average, and 47% won't use a business with fewer than 20
  reviews. For a landscaping company competing on trust with a stranger in
  their yard, that review count and freshness (74% of consumers prioritize
  reviews from the last three months, per the same survey) is a marketing
  asset, not a vanity metric.
- Seasonality drives search volume hard. Spring cleanup and mulch searches
  spike in March through May; hardscape and outdoor living searches build
  through late spring into summer as homeowners plan for entertaining
  season; snow removal and holiday lighting flip the seasonal script for
  companies that offer it. (`unverified` : exact monthly search curves need
  a keyword tool pull per market; see `keyword-seed.json`.)
- Commercial buyers move on a slower, RFP-anchored clock. Their journey
  starts with a bad experience from the current vendor, not a Google
  search; website and reviews matter less than a case study a property
  manager can forward to a board.

## What is usually broken

Ordered roughly by how often it shows up in a first audit:

1. **The Google Business Profile is thin or unclaimed-in-spirit.** Missing
   categories, no recent photos, hours wrong for the season, owner never
   responds to reviews. Google's own guidance is direct about what this
   costs: "Businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to
   show up in local search results" and "More reviews and positive ratings
   can help your business's local ranking" (support.google.com/business/answer/7091,
   seen 2026-09-02). This is the single highest-leverage, lowest-cost fix in
   the vertical.
2. **The website is a brochure, not a lead machine.** A logo, a service
   list, a contact form nobody checks. No mobile-fast load, no clear next
   step, no proof above the fold. BigOrange's own page claims a well-built
   online presence gets "up to 3x more leads than those without"
   (bigorange.marketing/landscaping-marketing-services/, seen 2026-09-02).
   Cite it as BigOrange's own claimed multiplier, not an independently
   verified figure.
3. **No review engine.** Great work, zero systematic ask. The crew does the
   job and leaves; nobody texts the homeowner a review link that night while
   the yard still looks amazing.
4. **No photo proof pipeline.** Before/afters exist on someone's phone and
   never make it to the website, GBP, or social. For a visual trade like
   landscaping this is a bigger miss than almost any other industry: the
   product is a photograph waiting to be taken.
5. **Quoting and follow-up leak leads.** A form comes in, nobody calls back
   for two days, the homeowner already booked someone else. Especially
   fatal for design-build, where the first response sets the trust tone for
   a five-figure purchase.
6. **No measurement.** The owner can't say which channel brought which job.
   Ad spend and SEO work happen on faith, not on a lead-to-close number tied
   back to a source.

## Objections and answers

- **"We get all our work from referrals, we don't need marketing."**
  Referrals are proof the work is good; they're not a growth plan you can
  turn a dial on. Ask what happens to the pipeline the month referrals slow
  down (a competitor undercuts, a slow season, a bad review sits unanswered).
  Marketing systematizes the trust referrals already prove exists.
- **"We tried Google Ads / SEO before and it didn't work."** Ask what was
  measured. Most "it didn't work" stories in this vertical trace back to
  point 6 above (no attribution) or point 5 (leads came in and nobody
  called back fast enough to close them). The fix is process, not abandoning
  the channel.
- **"We're too busy for marketing right now."** That's the moment the
  system pays off most: busy season is exactly when reviews, photos, and
  before/afters should be getting captured for the slow season content bank.
  Reframe as "we'll build it so it runs while your plate's already full,"
  which is BigOrange's own preferred language ("off your plate").
- **"Our website already looks fine."** Looks fine and converts are two
  different claims. Ask what it does for a homeowner who lands on it at
  9pm worried about a dead lawn: does it answer the worry, show proof, and
  make the next step obvious? A "cutie website" (BigOrange's own term) that
  doesn't convert is still a broken website.

## Proof patterns that work

- **Named before/after photo pairs**, captioned with the actual job type and
  neighborhood, not stock imagery. This is the single strongest proof asset
  in the vertical because the product is a visible before-and-after.
- **Review count and recency shown, not just claimed.** A live badge or
  quoted count beats "we have great reviews" every time, per the BrightLocal
  recency and volume findings above.
- **A named case study with a real number and a real name**: BigOrange's
  own cited example: a luxury landscaping firm that added a fourth crew
  after "Spring Cleanup bookings sold out within the first month"
  (bigorange.marketing/landscaping-marketing-services/, seen 2026-09-02).
  Use this pattern (specific season, specific outcome, specific business
  consequence) as the template for any new case study; never invent one
  in this shape for a client BigOrange hasn't actually served.
- **Neighborhood or municipality specificity** ("serving [named suburb]")
  reads as more credible than a broad service-area claim, because it mirrors
  how a homeowner actually searches ("landscaper near [suburb]").
- **Third-party award or directory badges** where genuinely earned:
  BigOrange's own "Best in Cincy" and Expertise.com badges are the kind of
  social proof pattern to replicate for a landscaping client's own local
  awards, never to imply the agency's award belongs to the client.

## KPIs and measurement ladder

Build the reporting conversation in this order; each rung only matters once
the one below it is solid:

1. **Visibility:** Google Business Profile views, map-pack ranking for
   named local queries, organic sessions to the site.
2. **Response:** call tracking numbers, form submissions, average
   time-to-first-response on a new lead (this is the leak point in "what is
   usually broken" #5, so it deserves its own line, not a rollup).
3. **Conversion:** estimate requests booked, close rate on estimates,
   average job value by lead source.
4. **Retention** (maintenance/commercial only): contract renewal rate,
   route density, customer lifetime value.
5. **Proof supply:** reviews collected per month, before/after sets
   captured per month. Treat this as a KPI in its own right, not an
   afterthought, because it feeds every other rung.

## Compliance cautions

- **Before/after photo permissions.** Confirm the client has the homeowner's
  or property owner's permission to use job-site photos publicly before they
  go on a website, GBP, or ad. This applies doubly for commercial/HOA work,
  where a property manager may require sign-off before any exterior photo of
  the property is published.
- **Licensing and certification claims.** Never state or imply a license,
  certification (e.g. a specific irrigation or pesticide/herbicide
  applicator license), or insurance status the client hasn't confirmed in
  writing. Licensing requirements vary by state and by service (pesticide
  application in particular is commonly regulated); don't assume a landscaper
  is licensed for chemical application just because they do maintenance.
- **Pesticide/fertilizer claims.** Avoid unverified environmental or safety
  claims ("pet-safe," "eco-friendly") unless the client can substantiate them
. This is a common regulatory and trust trip-wire in lawn care marketing.
- **Review solicitation.** Any review-generation tactic must comply with the
  review platform's own policies (no incentivized reviews, no review-gating
  that filters negative feedback away from the public platform). Google's
  own guidance frames legitimate review growth as service quality plus
  asking, not gaming.
